MeshCore Regions — Network Design and Operations Guide

A practical guide for people who run repeaters and design mesh networks. It covers what regions do, how to think about them when planning coverage, every CLI command with worked examples, and how to diagnose the failures they cause.

Copied 2026-08-07 from REGIONS_USER_GUIDE.md in the MeshCore working notes. Not an official MeshCore document.

Applies to: MeshCore firmware v1.16 and the versions leading up to it. Regions arrived in v1.10, region list in v1.12, default scope in v1.15. Where a feature has a minimum version it is noted inline.

Audience: repeater and room-server operators, and anyone designing a regional or metro-scale MeshCore network. Client-app users only need §3 and §11.

1. The problem regions solve#

MeshCore has two ways to move a packet.

Direct packets carry an explicit list of repeaters to travel through. They are cheap, precise, and touch only the repeaters on the path. Every established conversation uses direct routing.

Flood packets have no path yet, so every repeater that hears one rebroadcasts it. That is how a node announces itself, how the first message to a new contact gets through, and how paths get discovered in the first place. It is also, unavoidably, how one node's traffic ends up transmitted by every repeater within reach — including repeaters two counties away that will never be useful to anyone involved.

On a small mesh this is fine. As a network grows the flood traffic grows with it, and because every repeater shares one channel, a flood in one city costs airtime in the next. Left alone, a successful network throttles itself: adverts and first-contact messages saturate the band, and the direct traffic that actually carries conversations has to compete with it.

Regions put a boundary on flooding. A node stamps its flood packets with a region name. A repeater is configured with the list of regions it is willing to flood. If a packet's stamp isn't on the list, that repeater drops it instead of rebroadcasting.

The result is that a metro area's discovery traffic stays in the metro area, while established conversations still route anywhere the topology allows.

2. How regions work#

2.1 The stamp#

A region is just a name — chicago, wa-state, bay-area.

When your node sends a flood packet in a region, it computes a small stamp from that region's name and the packet's contents, and attaches it to the packet. The stamp costs 4 extra bytes of airtime.

A repeater that receives a stamped flood packet checks it against each region it is configured to carry. Match found → rebroadcast. No match → drop, silently.

Nothing else about the packet changes. The stamp is not an address, not a destination, and not visible to the recipient. It only tells repeaters along the way whether to keep passing the packet along.

2.2 Unstamped packets and the wildcard#

A packet sent without a region is unscoped. Every repeater has a special entry called the wildcard, written *, that governs unscoped traffic:

  • * allowed → the repeater floods unscoped packets, exactly as it did before regions existed.
  • * denied → the repeater drops every unscoped flood packet.

The wildcard is what makes a region rollout gradual. Leave it allowed and your network behaves as it always has while people opt into regions. Deny it, and you've made regions mandatory on that repeater.

2.3 What regions do not touch#

Direct packets are never filtered by region. Once two nodes know a path to each other, their traffic routes on that path regardless of what regions anyone has configured. A repeater in the middle forwards it even if it carries no region in common with either party.

This is worth stating twice, because it drives most of the practical advice in this guide:

Regions only ever affect discovery and first contact. They never break an established conversation.

Regions also don't touch message encryption, contact privacy, channels, or the radio settings (frequency, bandwidth, spreading factor). Two nodes on different radio settings can't hear each other at all; regions are a layer above that and assume everyone is already on the same channel.

2.4 Regions are not security#

The stamp is derived from the region's name by a public, published method. Anyone who knows the name can produce valid stamps for it, and anyone who guesses the name can confirm that a packet belongs to it.

A region is a traffic management convention, in the same spirit as an agreed channel name. It is not a password, not a private network, and not access control. Design accordingly, and never describe a region to users as something that keeps their traffic private.

(The firmware reserves a syntax for cryptographically private regions — names beginning with $ — but the key storage behind it is unimplemented in v1.16. A $ region silently matches nothing. Don't use them yet.)

3. What this means if you just use the app#

You don't configure regions on your phone or your companion radio the way an operator does on a repeater. Your client has two knobs:

Default scope. One region that stamps your outgoing flood traffic — your adverts, and your first message to a contact whose path you don't know yet. Recent app versions expose this in settings; the client can also discover the list of regions a nearby repeater will carry, so you can usually pick from a list rather than typing a name.

Per-conversation scope. Some clients let you override the region for a specific contact or channel.

Three things to know:

  1. Your adverts always use your default scope, never a per-conversation override. Your default scope is what determines who can discover you. Set it to something broad — the region your whole metro or state uses — not the narrowest one you can find.
  2. Once you've messaged someone successfully, regions stop mattering for that conversation. Path established, direct routing, done.
  3. If you set a default scope no nearby repeater carries, you go silent. Your messages leave your radio and die at the first repeater. Nothing tells you this happened.

If you're unsure, ask your local network operator what to set, or leave the default scope unset until you know.

4. The four settings on a repeater#

Every repeater, room server, and sensor node has a region table plus four things you can set. Getting these straight is most of the battle.

4.1 The region list and each region's flood flag#

The table holds up to 32 named regions. Each one is independently flagged either allowed or denied for flooding.

  • Allowed — the repeater rebroadcasts flood packets stamped with this region.
  • Denied — the repeater drops them.

A region that isn't in the table at all behaves identically to one that's denied. Adding a region and denying it is a way to keep a name on file (so it shows in your tree, so you can flip it on quickly) without carrying its traffic.

4.2 The wildcard flag#

* allowed or denied, governing unscoped flood traffic. See §2.2.

4.3 Default scope#

The region this node stamps its own flood packets with — principally its self-adverts.

This is the setting operators most often get wrong, because it looks like it configures what the repeater accepts. It doesn't. It configures what the repeater sends.

A repeater whose default scope is a region its neighbours don't carry will still forward everyone else's traffic perfectly, still answer anyone who has a path to it, and still look completely healthy on its own console — while its adverts propagate nowhere and nobody new can discover it.

Set the default scope to the broadest region your neighbours all carry. If you're not sure, leave it unset: unset means the repeater's adverts go out unscoped, which any repeater with * allowed will carry.

4.4 Home region#

A label. Set it to the region this node considers itself to live in, and it's marked with ^ in the region tree so you can see at a glance where a node belongs when you're reviewing configs.

In v1.16 the home region has no effect on packet handling. It's documentation, and a placeholder for a planned return-path feature. Set it if it helps you keep track; don't expect behaviour from it.

5. Six rules that decide everything#

Nearly every region question resolves to one of these.

Rule 1 — One stamp per packet, for its entire journey#

A flood packet carries exactly one region, applied by the sender, unchanged all the way to wherever it stops. There is no gateway, no boundary translation, no re-stamping.

So a packet gets from A to B only if there is a chain of repeaters from A to B where every single one carries that one region. A single repeater in the middle that doesn't carry it is a wall.

This is the most important rule in the document. Region coverage must be continuous along the path, not merely present at both ends.

Its corollary: first contact always requires a flood that survives the entire route. There is no other bootstrap. Importing a contact out of band gives you their identity but not a path, so the first message still floods. Only once a path exists does routing become direct and region-independent (§2.3).

Its other corollary: a region carried on some repeaters but not others doesn't degrade gracefully — it just draws a wall wherever the coverage stops. Nothing about a repeater's configuration can make traffic appear in a region; only senders create traffic in a region, and only their scope setting decides which one.

Rule 2 — Regions are a flat list, not a hierarchy#

The CLI displays regions as an indented tree, and you can nest them. The nesting is purely visual. It organises your display and nothing else.

Concretely:

  • Allowing europe does not allow its children uk or france.
  • A packet stamped europe does not match a repeater that carries only uk.
  • There is no relationship whatsoever between a parent's stamp and a child's stamp.

If you want a repeater to carry both a wide region and a narrow one, list both and allow both. That's what "overlapping regions" means in practice. Use the tree to keep 32 entries readable, and never rely on it for behaviour.

Rule 3 — Region names are case-sensitive and exact#

Chicago, chicago, and CHICAGO are three unrelated regions producing three unrelated stamps. There is no normalisation and no warning. A single node with the wrong capitalisation is invisible to everyone else and will look, from its own side, like the network is broken.

Agree on one spelling and write it down. See §7.

Rule 4 — Nothing is saved until you save it#

allowf, denyf, put, remove, home, def, and load all change the running configuration only. Reboot or lose power before region save and every change is gone.

The one exception is region default, which saves itself immediately.

The upside: if you lock yourself out with a bad denyf, a power cycle undoes it.

Rule 5 — Adverts use the default scope, always#

Not a per-conversation override, not the region of whatever prompted them. Adverts — the packets that make a node discoverable — are always stamped with the sender's default scope, or unscoped if none is set.

Discoverability is therefore governed entirely by default scope. This applies to repeaters advertising themselves and to client nodes alike.

Rule 6 — Repeaters reply in the region they were asked in; clients don't#

If you reach a repeater or room server with a request stamped chicago, its reply comes back stamped chicago. This is deliberate, and it's why you can't lock yourself out of a repeater by admin-ing it from an unusual region.

Client nodes do not do this yet. When one user's client replies to another user's first message, it stamps the reply with its own default scope, not the region the message arrived in. If the two users have different default scopes, the message arrives and the acknowledgement doesn't. See §11.1.

6. Designing a network#

Regions are the only tool MeshCore gives you for shaping flood propagation, and the design decisions are almost entirely social and geographic rather than technical. This section is the core of the guide.

6.1 Start from the failure you're preventing#

Don't add regions because they exist. Add them when flood traffic from areas that will never usefully talk to each other is measurably costing you airtime — typically when a network spans multiple metros linked by a few long-haul repeaters, or when adjacent independent communities can hear each other.

If your whole network fits in one metro and airtime is fine, the correct region configuration is: allow the wildcard, define nothing, and revisit later. A region scheme that nobody needs is pure operational overhead and a steady source of "why can't I message X" tickets.

Signals that it's time:

  • Adverts alone are a visible fraction of channel occupancy.
  • You can hear a neighbouring community's repeaters, and their discovery traffic is being rebroadcast by yours.
  • Your network spans more than one natural travel/social area, linked by a small number of long-haul sites.
  • New-contact messages routinely fail during busy periods while established conversations stay fine — a sign flood is congesting.

Signals it's premature:

  • Under ~20 repeaters in one contiguous area.
  • Airtime headroom is comfortable.
  • You don't yet have a way to reach your users with configuration instructions. Regions require user action; without a communication channel you'll strand people.

6.2 What makes a good region boundary#

A region should map to a set of people who plausibly want to discover each other. Not to geography for its own sake, not to administrative units, and not to who owns which repeater.

Ask three questions about any proposed boundary:

1. Would a typical user need to message across it? If yes, and often, it's the wrong boundary — every such conversation becomes a first-contact failure. Regions are cheap for traffic that never needed to cross, and expensive for traffic that does.

2. Is there a natural radio boundary that already exists? Mountain ranges, water gaps, and long unpopulated stretches make excellent region boundaries because flood traffic barely crosses them anyway. Drawing a region boundary along one costs you nothing and formalises what physics is already doing. Drawing one across a dense, well-connected urban area fights the topology and produces constant complaints.

3. Can you enumerate every repeater on the inside? If you can't list them, you can't guarantee coverage continuity, and the region will have holes (§6.4).

The single best predictor of a workable region is that it corresponds to something people already have a name for and already think of as one place — a metro area, a valley, an island, a county cluster. If you have to explain the boundary, it's probably wrong.

6.3 The umbrella pattern#

The pattern that works, and the one every successful deployment converges on:

One broad region that every repeater in your network carries. State-wide, metro-wide — whatever scope corresponds to "people here might reasonably want to talk to each other". This is the umbrella.

Everyone's default scope is the umbrella. Discovery works everywhere inside it. Nothing leaks to the neighbouring network.

Then, optionally, narrower regions on top. A county, a valley, a campus. Repeaters in that area carry both the umbrella and the local region (Rule 2 — you list both). Traffic that genuinely only concerns that area can be stamped with the local region and stays there.

* denied
umbrella: wa-state          allowed on every repeater
  local:  wa-seattle        allowed on Seattle-area repeaters only
          wa-spokane        allowed on Spokane-area repeaters only
          wa-olympic        allowed on Olympic Peninsula repeaters only

The umbrella is what makes the network work. The local regions are an optimisation. Get the umbrella right first and add local regions only when you can point at the traffic they'll save.

Why the umbrella has to be genuinely universal. Because a packet carries one stamp for its whole journey (Rule 1), the umbrella is the only region guaranteed to reach anyone. It's what users set as their default scope, so it's what their adverts and first-contact messages use. A repeater that doesn't carry the umbrella isn't "partially participating" — it's a wall that everyone's discovery traffic dies against. One umbrella, on every repeater, no exceptions is the whole design.

How many layers? Two is almost always right: umbrella plus optional local. Three layers (state / metro / neighbourhood) very rarely pays for itself — the third layer's traffic is small, its coverage is hard to guarantee, and it multiplies the ways a user can pick a scope that doesn't work. If you're reaching for a third layer, check whether you actually want two independent networks with separate umbrellas (§6.8).

6.4 Coverage continuity — the constraint that shapes every design#

From Rule 1: a region is only useful along a path where every repeater carries it. Not most. Every.

This is the constraint that determines whether a region design works, and it's the one that people from other mesh protocols consistently underestimate, because there is no fallback, no downgrade, and no error. A packet reaching a repeater that doesn't carry its region simply ceases to exist, and the sender is told nothing.

Designing to it:

  1. List the repeaters, don't sketch a shape on a map. A region is defined by a set of specific repeaters, not by a boundary. If you can't name them, the design isn't finished.
  2. Trace the paths, not the area. Take the pairs of users you expect to talk. For each, trace the actual repeater chain. Every repeater on every chain must carry the region.
  3. Watch for single points of dependency. If exactly one repeater links two halves of a region, that repeater's configuration is the region. When it reboots into a stale config, the region silently splits in two.
  4. Check the edges of coverage, not the centre. The centre always works. Failures live at the fringe, where a user's only route is via one marginal site.

Practical rule: never define a region whose intended coverage area spans a repeater you don't control, unless you've agreed it with that operator first — and confirmed they'll keep carrying it after their next firmware update or factory reset.

Redundancy note. A region with only one path between its halves is fragile in a way that has nothing to do with radio. Where two areas are linked by several repeaters, make sure all of them carry the region, not just the one you tested through. Partial coverage on redundant links produces the worst failure mode there is: intermittent, path-dependent, and impossible to reproduce.

6.5 Backbone and linking repeaters#

Long-haul, hilltop, and inter-city repeaters need their own policy, because they're the sites where a region design succeeds or fails.

Their defining property is that they're on the path between areas, which means every region that needs to cross carries through them. In practice:

  • Backbone sites should carry every umbrella they bridge, including neighbouring networks' umbrellas if users are ever expected to cross. They're the crossing points; that's their job.
  • Backbone sites should generally not carry local sub-regions. A local region's whole purpose is to stay local; if the backbone carries it, it propagates to the far end and you've achieved nothing.
  • Backbone sites are where flood.max earns its keep. A hilltop repeater with enormous coverage can, on its own, take a local flood network-wide. Capping hop counts there limits the blast radius of any misconfiguration elsewhere.
  • Their default scope matters most. A backbone repeater whose adverts don't propagate is invisible to new users on both sides of the link, which is the most damaging place for that particular mistake to happen (§4.3).

If a backbone site is operated by someone outside your group — very common, since the good hilltops tend to be shared — treat its configuration as a fixed external constraint and design around what it carries, rather than assuming it will change.

6.6 Topology archetypes#

Most real networks are a variation on one of these.

Single metro, contiguous coverage

20–100 repeaters, everyone plausibly wants to reach everyone.

Design: one region, allowed on every repeater. Everyone's default scope is it. Deny the wildcard once adoption is high, or soft-limit it.

Don't subdivide. The entire population is one discovery community; every internal boundary you draw is a boundary someone's conversation has to cross. The only reason to have a region at all is to keep your neighbours' flood traffic out and yours out of theirs.

Two metros linked by a corridor

Two dense areas, a thin chain of repeaters between them.

Design: an umbrella covering both, carried everywhere including the corridor. Optionally one local region per metro, carried only within that metro and not on the corridor repeaters.

Most users set the umbrella. Users who only ever talk locally can set their metro region and save the corridor the traffic. The corridor repeaters carry the umbrella only, which is exactly what confines local traffic.

This is the archetype where local sub-regions genuinely pay, because the corridor is a real bottleneck and there is real traffic that has no business crossing it.

Statewide, several metros, sparse between

Design: state umbrella everywhere; one region per metro; nothing else. Backbone sites carry the umbrella only.

Resist per-county regions. The population outside the metros is small, their traffic is small, and each extra region is another way for a user to end up in a scope that doesn't reach anyone.

Isolated pocket — island, valley, remote town

A cluster reachable through one or two links, or not at all.

Design: if it's genuinely isolated, it needs no region — nothing is leaking either way, and adding one only creates a way to fail. If it's linked, give it the umbrella like everywhere else, and let its physical isolation do the traffic-limiting that a region would otherwise do.

This is the case where operators most often add a region that costs them support load and saves them nothing.

Dense core inside a wider area

A downtown with heavy repeater density inside a metro that has an umbrella.

Design: umbrella everywhere, plus a core region on the dense repeaters. High-volume local chatter can be scoped to the core.

Set expectations clearly: a user who sets the core region as their default scope becomes undiscoverable to the rest of the metro (Rule 5). The core region is for per-conversation or per-channel scoping, and the default scope stays the umbrella. If your client apps can't do per-conversation scoping, don't build this pattern.

Temporary or event network

A festival, an exercise, a deployment.

Design: one dedicated region on the event repeaters, plus the local umbrella if the site is inside an existing network. Retire it afterwards with the deny-then-remove sequence (§9.7).

Pick a name that can't be confused with a permanent region and won't be reused next year — including the year (fieldday-2026) is the one case where dating a name is right, because the region is genuinely disposable.

Multiple independent communities in radio range

Separate groups, separate operators, overlapping coverage.

Design: each community runs its own umbrella and denies the others'. If crossing needs to be routine, the honest answer is a single shared umbrella across both — a border-only "interconnect" region does not work, for reasons worth reading before you plan this. See §6.8.

6.7 Node types and what each needs#

Two design consequences:

Room servers must be reachable by the people meant to use them. A room server's default scope determines who can discover it, and the regions it carries determine whose first-contact messages reach it. A room intended for a whole metro should sit on the umbrella, even if the repeater it's co-located with is doing something narrower.

Sensors are senders, not gatekeepers. A sensor's region setting affects only whether its own traffic gets carried. Set its default scope to whatever region reaches its collector, and don't worry about its region list.

6.8 Borders and interconnect#

Two neighbouring networks that can hear each other should each deny the other's regions, so routine flood traffic stays home. That part is straightforward and entirely under operator control.

Letting users cross the border is not, and this is the design question people get wrong most often.

Why "put a shared region on the border repeaters" doesn't work

Carrying a region on a repeater controls only what that repeater forwards. It never causes anything to be sent in that region. So a shared region configured on the border repeaters and nowhere else, with no user stamping packets with it, is completely inert — it permits a class of traffic that doesn't exist.

There is no operator-side action that creates cross-border traffic. Specifically:

  • Adverts are stamped by the sender, with the sender's default scope (Rule 5). No border-repeater configuration can make network B's adverts cross into network A. If nobody in B is advertising in the shared region, nobody in A will ever discover them.
  • Setting the shared region as the border repeaters' own default scope does not help. A repeater's default scope stamps only its own adverts. That governs whether users can discover and administer that repeater — it has nothing to do with whether other people's traffic crosses it. Routing does not require discovering the repeaters you route through; paths are built by flood regardless of whether you have those repeaters as contacts.
  • First contact always requires a flood that survives the entire route. There is no other bootstrap. Until a path exists, every message floods, and that flood carries one stamp end to end.
What is actually required

For a user in network A and a user in network B to establish contact through a shared region X:

  1. X is carried continuously along the whole path — every repeater from the first one the sender can hear, through the border, to the last one before the recipient. Not just the border repeaters. Any single repeater on the route that doesn't carry X ends it.
  2. The sender is transmitting in X — via default scope, or a per-conversation override if their client supports one.
  3. The recipient's default scope is also X. This is the strict one. Their client replies to a first message in its own scope, not the scope the message arrived in (Rule 6) — it has no way to know it should answer in X. So the recipient must already have been configured for X before ever hearing from the sender. Without this the message lands, no acknowledgement returns, no path is established, and the sender's client retries forever.
  4. Discovery needs the recipient advertising in X too, since adverts use default scope only — or contacts exchanged out of band, which solves discovery but not points 1–3.
The consequence

Once both populations must set X as their default scope, and X must be carried across the interior of both networks rather than just at the border, X is a shared umbrella. It was never a separate mechanism. Calling it an "interconnect" describes an intention, not a different technical arrangement.

So there are really only two honest designs:

A. Genuinely shared coverage. Adopt one umbrella across both networks, carried everywhere, and have everyone use it as their default scope. Keep each network's local regions for local traffic. This is what you want if cross-network contact is expected to be routine. The two groups are, for discovery purposes, one network — say so.

B. Deliberately separate networks. Each keeps its own umbrella and denies the other's. Cross-network contact is exceptional and handled per-case: a user who needs it temporarily switches their default scope to the other network's umbrella (which the border repeaters must carry for this to work at all), establishes contact, and switches back. Once a path exists it's direct-routed and regions stop mattering permanently (§2.3) — so the awkward step is needed only once per contact pair.

Design B is legitimate and often correct — it accurately reflects two communities that mostly don't interact. What doesn't work is the middle option people reach for first: a shared region on the border repeaters that nobody's default scope points at.

If you do run a shared region
  • Name it for its purpose (cascadia-link, metro-interop), so a future operator doing a cleanup doesn't remove something they don't recognise.
  • Publish it as a default-scope instruction to users, not as a repeater configuration note. The user-side setting is the part that makes it function.
  • Carry it deep enough on both sides to reach the users who need it, and be explicit about how deep — "border repeaters only" is almost never useful, since it serves only users in direct radio range of those repeaters who have already set it as their default.
  • Expect it to become an umbrella if it's used routinely, and plan for that rather than being surprised by it.
The one real bypass

If a client lets a user set a contact's path manually, and they know the repeater chain, that skips flood entirely and works across any region boundary. It's a manual, expert-level operation and not something to build a network on, but it's worth knowing when you need to get two specific people connected across a border today.

6.9 The region budget, and growing into it#

32 regions per node is generous for a well-designed network and tight for a badly-designed one. A healthy metro repeater carries 2–4. If any node is approaching double figures, the design has drifted — usually into per-town or per-operator regions that should have been one umbrella.

Splitting a region as the network grows. When one region gets too large, don't renumber everything. Introduce the new narrower regions alongside the existing one, on the relevant repeaters, and leave the old region in place as the umbrella. Users migrate gradually or not at all, and nothing breaks in the meantime. The old wide region becoming the umbrella is the natural evolution, not a problem to fix.

Merging two regions. Have every repeater carry both for a transition period, announce the survivor, then deny and later remove the retired one (§9.7). Never remove first — users still stamping with the dead region go silent with no diagnostic.

Renaming. There's no rename. It's an add, a migration period, and a removal — with a capitalisation trap waiting in the middle. Get names right the first time (§7).

6.10 Multi-operator governance#

Region schemes span administrative boundaries, and the technical design is the easy half.

Agree in advance, in writing, on:

  • The exact spelling of every region name, capitalisation included. This is the single highest-value line in your documentation.
  • Which repeaters carry which regions, as a list, kept current.
  • What users should set as their default scope — one answer, not a menu.
  • Who can create a region, and how a change is announced. Uncoordinated region creation is how a network ends up with seattle, Seattle, and wa-seattle all half-deployed.
  • What happens on a factory reset. A repeater rebuilt from defaults carries nothing and allows the wildcard. Someone needs to notice and re-apply.

The failure mode to design against isn't malice, it's drift: an operator reflashes a site, doesn't restore the region config, and a hole opens in a region that nobody notices until a user reports that messages to one part of town don't work.

6.11 The soft alternative: hop limits on unscoped traffic#

Denying the wildcard is a hard cutoff — legacy nodes, visitors, and anyone who hasn't configured a region simply stop working through your repeater.

There's a gentler option. Repeaters have a separate hop limit that applies only to unscoped flood packets:

set flood.max.unscoped 3

Unscoped traffic now propagates up to 3 hops and stops. Local, unconfigured users still work in their immediate area; a distant network's unscoped flood no longer crosses yours.

Three related hop limits exist:

SettingApplies toDefault
flood.maxall flood packets64
flood.max.unscopedunscoped flood packets only64
flood.max.advertflood adverts8

flood.max.advert is worth knowing about independently: adverts are the single largest category of flood traffic on a busy mesh, and capping them at a few hops is often the highest-value change you can make before touching regions at all.

Consider this ordering when a network starts to congest:

  1. Tighten flood.max.advert.
  2. Add flood.max.unscoped.
  3. Introduce an umbrella region, wildcard still allowed.
  4. Deny the wildcard once adoption is high enough.

Each step is reversible and each is less disruptive than the one after it.

6.12 Airtime economics#

Scoping a packet costs 4 bytes. On a slow spreading factor that is not nothing, but it's small against a typical payload — and it's trivial against the cost of that packet being rebroadcast by thirty repeaters that had no reason to.

The saving is proportional to how much of your network a flood doesn't reach. A region that excludes 10% of your repeaters is barely worth the operational cost. One that excludes 80% pays for itself immediately.

A useful way to size the win before committing: for a typical flood packet today, count how many repeaters retransmit it. Then count how many would under your proposed design. The ratio is your saving, and if it isn't dramatic, the region isn't worth the support burden.

Note also what regions don't save. They don't reduce direct traffic, which on a mature network is most of it. They reduce discovery overhead. If your congestion is conversations rather than adverts and first contacts, regions are the wrong tool and you need more spectrum, tighter hop limits, or fewer overlapping repeaters.

6.13 Occasional false matches#

Stamps are small — 16 bits. A packet from an unrelated region has roughly a 1 in 65,000 chance of accidentally matching any given region a repeater checks. A repeater carrying 30 regions will therefore forward something it shouldn't every few thousand packets.

This is a designed trade-off and needs no action. It's worth knowing only so that a single inexplicably-forwarded packet in a log doesn't send you hunting for a misconfiguration.

6.14 Validating a design before you deploy#

Regions fail silently, so testing has to be deliberate. Before rolling a scheme out:

On paper

  1. For each region, list every repeater that will carry it. If the list is vague, stop.
  2. Pick the three or four longest user-to-user paths inside each region and confirm every repeater on them is on that list.
  3. Confirm every repeater carries the umbrella. Every one.
  4. Confirm every repeater's default scope is a region its neighbours carry.
  5. Count regions per node. More than four on a normal site is a smell.

In the field

  1. Test discovery, not messaging. Regions only affect flood, so a working conversation proves nothing. Factory-fresh or path-reset a client, set its default scope, and check it can find and first-contact a node at the far end of the region.
  2. Test from the fringe, not from next to a repeater.
  3. Test in both directions. Because clients reply in their own scope rather than the sender's (Rule 6), a one-way test can pass while the return path fails — and that's the most common real-world failure.
  4. Test with the wildcard denied somewhere before you deny it everywhere. One repeater is a safe experiment; a fleet is not.
  5. Reboot a repeater and re-test. This catches configuration that was never saved (Rule 4), which is otherwise found weeks later during a power cut.

Recovery plan

Have one before you start: know which command undoes each change, and remember that anything not yet saved is undone by a power cycle. region allowf * without saving is the universal panic button (§9.6).

6.15 A worked design#

A regional group covering a state with two population centres, an inter-city corridor, and a neighbouring group one state over.

Inventory

  • 40 repeaters in the northern metro, dense.
  • 25 in the southern metro, dense.
  • 6 along the corridor between them, two of which are shared hilltop sites owned by a third party.
  • 3 near the southern border, within earshot of the neighbouring group.

Decisions

Resulting per-node configuration

  * F                              * F
   nw-state^ F                      nw-state^ F
    nw-north F

Southern metro repeater          Border repeater
  * F                              * F
   nw-state^ F                      nw-state^ F
    nw-south F                       nw-south F
                                     sw-state F      <- neighbours' umbrella

Regions per node: 2–3. Every node carries the umbrella. No node's default scope is narrower than the umbrella. Local traffic is confined by the corridor sites not carrying the metro regions, which is exactly where the traffic needed to stop.

Cross-border contact is occasional here, so there is no shared region. A user who needs someone in the neighbouring network temporarily sets their default scope to sw-state, makes contact, and switches back; the border repeaters carrying sw-state are what make that possible. If crossing later becomes routine, the two groups adopt one shared umbrella and stop pretending they're separate networks (§6.8).

What this design deliberately does not do: no per-county regions, no third layer, no region on the isolated communities beyond the umbrella, no dedicated interconnect region, and no attempt to use nesting for behaviour — nw-north is displayed under nw-state purely so the tree reads well, and it is allowed by its own explicit setting (Rule 2).

6.16 Documentation is part of the design#

A region scheme lives in the heads of every operator and every user in your network. Publish:

  • The exact spelling of every region name, with capitalisation.
  • Which repeaters carry which regions — as a list, kept current.
  • What users should set as their default scope. One answer.
  • Who to contact before adding a region, and how changes are announced.
  • The re-application checklist for a repeater rebuilt from defaults.

A wiki page or pinned message is enough. Networks fail at regions through drift and inconsistent spelling far more often than through bad design.

7. Naming regions#

Rules the firmware enforces:

  • Maximum 30 characters; longer names are silently truncated, which will break your stamps in a way that's very hard to see.
  • Letters, digits, and - are safe everywhere. So are # and $ as a leading character, where they have special meaning (below).
  • Spaces are not allowed. Neither are . , : ; / \ @ ( ) ! ? = + * and most other punctuation.
  • Case-sensitive (Rule 3).

A leading # is optional and cosmetic. chicago and #chicago are the same region, produce the same stamp, and interoperate. The CLI strips the # when displaying. You'll see both spellings in older documentation; it makes no difference. Pick one style for your own docs.

Avoid a leading $. It selects the private-region mechanism, which is unimplemented in v1.16 — such a region matches nothing and silently blackholes traffic.

Conventions worth adopting:

  • All lowercase, hyphen-separated. Eliminates the entire class of capitalisation bugs. wa-seattle, not WA_Seattle.
  • Prefix by scope level so the tree sorts and reads well: wa-state, wa-seattle, wa-spokane.
  • Don't encode the year, the operator, or the hardware. Regions outlive all three.
  • Don't reuse a retired name for a different area. Nodes cache configurations for a long time and old stamps will show up.
  • Keep names short. They're typed into phone apps by people standing outside in the cold.

8. CLI reference#

Region commands work on repeaters, room servers, and sensor nodes. They're available over the serial console, and over the air to an authenticated admin client.

Two notes before the commands:

  • Over-the-air replies are capped at about 160 characters. A large region tree will be cut off when you run region remotely. Use region list or check on serial.
  • Most commands match names by prefix. region denyf chi will happily act on chicago. Convenient when typing; dangerous in scripts. region remove is the exception — it requires the exact full name.

8.1 Viewing#

region

Prints the whole tree.

region
* F
 wa-state^ F
  wa-seattle F
  wa-spokane
 or-portland

Reading the output:

MarkerMeaning
F after the nameflood allowed
no Fflood denied
^ after the namethis is the home region
indentationtree position only — no behavioural meaning (Rule 2)
first line *the wildcard, governing unscoped traffic

So the example reads: unscoped traffic allowed; wa-state and wa-seattle carried; wa-spokane and or-portland known but not carried; home is wa-state.

The default scope is not shown here — check it with region default.

region list allowed / region list denied

(firmware 1.12+)

A comma-separated list, much shorter than the tree and safe to run over the air.

region list allowed
*,wa-state,wa-seattle

Prints -none- if the filter matches nothing.

region get <name>

Details for one region, including its parent.

region get wa-seattle
 wa-seattle (wa-state) F

Trailing F means flood allowed. No F means denied.

8.2 Creating and removing#

region put <name> [parent_name]

Creates a region. Created regions are flood-allowed immediately — put is an "allow this" action, not just a bookkeeping one.

region put wa-state
region put wa-seattle wa-state

Omitting the parent puts it at top level. If the name already exists, this moves it to a new parent instead of creating a duplicate. Remember the parent is cosmetic.

region def <token> [<token> ...]

(one-line tree builder)

Builds several regions in one command. A cursor starts at the top; each token creates a region under the cursor and moves the cursor to it. Add |target to jump the cursor somewhere else afterwards.

A straight chain, each nested under the previous:

region def a b c d e

A flat list — |* returns the cursor to the top after each:

region def wa-seattle|* wa-spokane|* wa-olympic

A branched tree:

region def wa-state wa-seattle|wa-state wa-spokane

gives wa-state at top level with wa-seattle and wa-spokane beneath it.

Notes:

  • Everything created is flood-allowed.
  • The jump target must already exist, or have been created earlier in the same command.
  • Serial input is limited to about 160 characters per line. Split large trees across several commands — the cursor resets to the top each time, so lead the next command with something|existing-parent to reposition.
  • A failure part-way through leaves everything created before it in place. Run region afterwards to check, and repair with put / remove.
  • Each token splits at most once, so region def a|b|c doesn't do what it looks like.
region remove <name>
region remove wa-spokane
  • Requires the exact full name — no prefix matching here.
  • Children must be removed first. Err - not empty means it still has children; remove the leaves and work upward.
  • The wildcard * can't be removed.

8.3 Allowing and denying#

region allowf <name> / region denyf <name>
region allowf wa-state       # carry flood traffic stamped wa-state
region denyf  or-portland    # stop carrying or-portland

Both accept *:

region allowf *              # carry unscoped flood traffic (the pre-regions default)
region denyf  *              # drop all unscoped flood traffic

Prefix matching applies. If both wa-seattle and wa-spokane exist, region denyf wa-s will hit one of them with no warning about the ambiguity. Type the full name.

8.4 Default scope and home#

region default / region default <name> / region default <null>

(firmware 1.15+)

Sets the region this node stamps its own outgoing flood packets with — mainly its adverts. See §4.3.

region default              # show current
region default wa-state     # set
region default <null>       # clear (adverts go out unscoped)

Behaviour worth knowing:

  • If the named region doesn't exist yet it's created automatically and flood-allowed.
  • This command saves immediately — no region save needed. It's the only region command that does.
  • region default * does not set the wildcard as your default scope. The wildcard isn't a real region, so this silently clears your default scope instead. Use <null> if that's what you meant, and a real region name otherwise.
region home / region home <name>
region home                 # show current
region home wa-seattle      # set

A label only in v1.16 — see §4.4. Shows as ^ in the tree.

8.5 Bulk load#

region load

Replaces the entire region table from a series of indented lines. Useful for pushing an identical configuration to a fleet of repeaters.

Read §8.6 before using this. It has side effects beyond the region list.

region load
 wa-state F
  wa-seattle F
  wa-spokane
 or-portland

region save

(The blank line at the end commits the load. Then save.)

Format rules:

  • One space of indentation per level, and top-level regions need exactly one leading space. A line with no indentation is silently ignored — this is by far the most common mistake with this command.
  • Append F to allow flooding. Without it, a newly created region is denied.
  • Regions that already exist keep their current flags, and the F on the line is ignored for them. To change a flag on an existing region use allowf / denyf, not load.
  • Maximum 7 levels of nesting.
  • Nothing is echoed back until the blank line commits, which over the air looks like the node has stopped responding. It hasn't.
  • Because a blank line is hard to send from some admin clients, this command is easiest on the serial console.

The output of region is valid input to region load — the wildcard line at column 0 is ignored, and its children are already indented by one. Copy, edit, paste.

8.6 What region load resets

Committing a region load also, as a side effect:

  • Clears the home region.
  • Clears the default scope.
  • Resets the wildcard to allowed — so a repeater that had region denyf * starts carrying unscoped flood traffic again after you save and reboot.

None of this is announced. After every region load, re-apply:

region denyf *              # if you had the wildcard denied
region home wa-seattle      # if you had a home set
region default wa-state     # if you had a default scope
region save

If you're only changing one or two regions, put / allowf / denyf are safer than a full load.

8.7 Saving#

region save
region save

Writes the region table to flash. Required after every command except region default (Rule 4).

Also marks the node as recently modified, so clients querying for changed nodes pick it up.

8.8 Related settings#

Not region commands, but part of the same toolkit:

get flood.max                    set flood.max <0-64>
get flood.max.unscoped           set flood.max.unscoped <0-64>
get flood.max.advert             set flood.max.advert <0-64>

See §6.11.

9. Recipes#

9.1 First region on a fresh repeater, nothing disrupted#

Carry a new region while everything that worked before keeps working.

region put wa-state
region default wa-state
region save
region

put allows it, the wildcard stays allowed so unscoped traffic is unaffected, and the default scope means this repeater's own adverts are stamped wa-state.

Only set the default scope once neighbouring repeaters also carry wa-state — otherwise this repeater's adverts stop propagating (§4.3). Safe ordering: roll out region put wa-state across the fleet first, then go back and set default scope.

9.2 Metro network with local sub-regions#

On every repeater in the state:

region put wa-state
region default wa-state
region save

Then on Seattle-area repeaters only:

region put wa-seattle wa-state
region save

and Spokane-area only:

region put wa-spokane wa-state
region save

Every repeater carries wa-state, so state-wide discovery works. Seattle repeaters carry wa-seattle in addition — the nesting under wa-state is display only, and put allowed it explicitly (Rule 2).

Users set their default scope to wa-state and optionally scope specific chatter to their local region.

9.3 Making regions mandatory#

Once adoption is high enough that unscoped traffic is mostly noise:

region list allowed
region denyf *
region save

Then watch. From this moment, anyone whose default scope isn't one of your allowed regions is cut off from discovery through this repeater. Established conversations continue unaffected (§2.3).

Softer alternative, worth trying first:

set flood.max.unscoped 3

Local unscoped users keep working; distant unscoped floods don't cross you.

9.4 Letting two neighbouring networks reach each other#

Read §6.8 first. Configuring a shared region on the border repeaters alone accomplishes nothing — the user-side default scope is what makes cross-border traffic exist, and the recipient's default scope has to match before they can even acknowledge a first message.

Option A — occasional contact, networks stay separate. No repeater changes at all beyond making sure the border repeaters carry both networks' umbrellas:

region put nw-state          # on network B's border repeaters
region put sw-state          # on network A's border repeaters
region save

A user who needs to reach someone across the border temporarily sets their default scope to the other network's umbrella, establishes contact, and switches back. Once the path exists it's direct-routed and regions stop mattering for that contact permanently (§2.3), so this is a one-time step per contact pair.

Option B — routine contact. Adopt a genuinely shared umbrella across both networks:

region put cascadia            # on every repeater in BOTH networks
region default cascadia
region save

Then publish cascadia to both communities as the default scope everyone should set. Local regions on each side continue to confine local traffic.

This is the honest version of "interconnect" when crossing is expected to be normal. Don't build it as a border-only region and hope — that configuration serves only users in direct radio range of the border repeaters who have already set it as their default.

Whichever you pick, publish the exact name to both communities before anyone configures anything. A capitalisation mismatch here produces two silently separate one-sided regions (Rule 3).

9.5 Cloning a configuration to a fleet#

On the reference repeater:

region

Copy the output. On each target, region load, paste the indented lines (drop the * line — it's ignored anyway), blank line, then:

region denyf *              # only if the reference had it denied
region home <name>
region default <name>
region save

The re-application step is mandatory — see §8.6.

9.6 Temporarily opening up to debug#

Regions are a plausible suspect whenever discovery misbehaves. To rule them out:

region allowf *

Deliberately don't save. Test. Then either save if that was the fix, or reboot the node to revert (Rule 4).

9.7 Retiring a region#

region denyf old-name
region save

Wait for users to migrate — days to weeks, since clients cache settings and people don't read announcements. Then:

region remove old-name
region save

Removing it outright while nodes still stamp with it produces the same result as denying it, but without a table entry you can point at when someone asks why their traffic vanished. Deny first, remove later.

10. Rolling regions out without breaking your network#

Regions are a coordination problem far more than a technical one. A staged rollout:

Stage 0 — Decide and publish. Names, exact spelling, which repeater carries what, what users should set. Nothing gets configured before this is written down. Cheapest stage and the one most often skipped.

Stage 1 — Reduce flood pressure without regions. Tighten flood.max.advert. Measure. This alone often defers the whole project.

Stage 2 — Define the umbrella everywhere, wildcard still allowed. region put <umbrella> on every repeater. Zero user-visible change; you're just making the network ready.

Stage 3 — Repeaters adopt the default scope. Only once Stage 2 is complete across the fleet, so no repeater's adverts fall into a hole.

Stage 4 — Users adopt the umbrella as their default scope. This is the long stage. Announce it repeatedly. Expect months, not weeks.

Stage 5 — Soft-limit unscoped traffic. set flood.max.unscoped 3. Unconfigured users still work locally; distant unscoped floods stop crossing you. This is where most of the airtime win actually lands, and for many networks it's the right place to stop.

Stage 6 — Deny the wildcard. Only if Stage 5 wasn't enough. Announce a date well in advance. This is the only irreversible-feeling step for your users, and the only one that generates support load.

Stage 7 — Add local sub-regions. Optional, and only where you can point at the traffic they'll save.

Skipping to Stage 6 is the classic mistake. It works technically and it strands every user who hasn't configured a region, all at once, with no error message on their end.

11. Troubleshooting#

Before anything else, confirm the basics: same frequency, bandwidth, and spreading factor; nodes in range; repeater forwarding enabled. Regions are a plausible cause only when radio-level connectivity is already confirmed.

The diagnostic signature of a region problem: flood-dependent things fail, direct things work. Discovery, first contact, and adverts break; anything with an established path is fine.

11.1 Messages arrive one-way, with no delivery confirmation#

Symptom. You message someone new. They receive it. You get no acknowledgement, no path is established, and your client keeps retrying.

Cause. You and they have different default scopes. Your message was stamped with yours and got through. Their reply was stamped with theirs — because client nodes reply in their own region, not the region the message came in (Rule 6) — and died on a repeater that doesn't carry it.

Fix. Both parties set their default scope to a common region that's carried continuously along the path between you — normally the umbrella. Once one message round-trips, the conversation is direct-routed and the problem is gone permanently.

Partial workaround. Exchanging contacts out of band — QR code, contact share, zero-hop share in person — solves discovery, but not this. Importing a contact gives you their identity, not a path to them, so your first message still floods and still needs a scope that survives the whole route. Out-of-band exchange removes one of the two flood-dependent steps, not both.

Full bypass. If your client lets you set a contact's path manually and you know the repeater chain, that skips flood entirely and is immune to region mismatch.

11.2 Nobody can discover my repeater#

Symptom. The repeater forwards traffic normally. Existing users reach it fine. New users never see it appear.

Cause. Its default scope is a region its neighbours don't carry, so its adverts die one hop out (§4.3). Everything else about it works, which is what makes this so hard to spot.

Fix.

region default

Compare against what neighbouring repeaters actually carry. Set it to the umbrella, or clear it with region default <null> if neighbours allow the wildcard.

11.3 Traffic stops at one specific repeater#

Symptom. Two areas can't reach each other. Both ends are configured identically and correctly.

Cause. A repeater in the middle doesn't carry the region (Rule 1). Frequently a hilltop or backbone site operated by someone else.

Fix. Trace the actual path repeater by repeater and check each one's region list allowed. Either get the intermediate site to carry the region, or use a region that it does carry — usually the umbrella.

11.4 Everything stopped when I denied the wildcard#

Symptom. region denyf * and discovery through that repeater dies completely.

Cause. Your users hadn't adopted a region yet, so all their traffic was unscoped (§10, Stage 6).

Fix.

region allowf *
region save

Then restart at Stage 4 and try set flood.max.unscoped 3 instead.

11.5 My region works for some people and not others#

Cause, in order of likelihood:

  1. Capitalisation mismatch (Rule 3). Have affected users read back their setting character by character.
  2. Name truncated past 30 characters — everyone who typed it in a client with a shorter field limit is in a different region.
  3. Coverage gap on their particular path (§11.3).
  4. A leading $ typo, which makes the region match nothing at all.

11.6 My changes disappeared after a reboot#

Cause. No region save (Rule 4).

Note that region default saves itself, which makes this more confusing — the default scope survives while your allowf and denyf changes don't.

11.7 Err - not empty#

region remove refuses while a region still has children in the tree. Remove the deepest ones first and work upward.

11.8 I changed a flag and the wrong region changed#

Prefix matching (§8). region denyf wa-s acted on whichever of wa-seattle / wa-spokane it found. Run region to see the damage, fix with full names.

11.9 region load didn't create anything#

The lines had no leading space. Top-level regions need exactly one (§8.5). Unindented lines are ignored silently.

11.10 Unscoped traffic came back on its own#

A region load reset the wildcard to allowed (§8.6). Re-apply region denyf * and save.

11.11 A remote region command returned truncated output#

Over-the-air replies cap at ~160 characters. Use region list allowed / region list denied, or read the full tree on serial.

12. Anti-patterns#

Relying on nesting for behaviour. Allowing a parent does not allow its children. Every region needs its own allowf. (Rule 2 — the single most common design error.)

Regions as privacy. Anyone who knows the name can produce and read the stamp. Never present a region to users as a private network.

A region per repeater, or per small town. With one stamp per packet and no translation, fine-grained regions mean nothing can cross anything. Regions should be about as coarse as "people here might want to talk to each other".

Denying the wildcard on day one. Instantly strands every user who hasn't configured a region, with no error visible on their end.

Setting a narrow default scope. Whether on a repeater or a client, the default scope determines discoverability. Narrow default scope = invisible. Default scope should be your broadest useful region; use per-conversation overrides for anything narrower.

Defining a region across infrastructure you don't control. You're depending on another operator to carry it, forever, without having asked.

Using region load for a small change. It resets the wildcard, home, and default scope. Use put / allowf / denyf.

Names with mixed case, or that vary between documents. Guaranteed silent partitions.

Leading $. Selects an unimplemented mechanism; the region matches nothing.

Deploying without publishing. The scheme has to live somewhere other than one operator's memory.

The six rules#

  1. One stamp per packet, unchanged end to end — coverage must be continuous along the path.
  2. Regions are a flat list; the tree is cosmetic.
  3. Names are case-sensitive and exact.
  4. Nothing persists until region save — except region default.
  5. Adverts always use the default scope.
  6. Repeaters reply in the region they were asked in; client nodes don't.
